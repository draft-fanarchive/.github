⏺ Short answer: your intuition holds, but not for the reason you gave. On
  expressive power, all three fit inside the regular languages. A regex over an
  alphabet of works falls short for two other reasons: it loses the reader's
  choice points, and it cannot describe "any order" compactly. The minimum you
  need is two kinds of sequence (a choice graph and a partial order) plus the
  nesting you already have.

  Where each example sits

  Work: Rayuela
  Natural shape: One finite list, plus one list ending in a cycle
  Language class over works: Regular. The loop makes it an infinite reading, but

  a trivial one. Your current Sequence (steps + loopTo) already handles it.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Work: CYOA
  Natural shape: Chapters linked by choices, with loops
  Language class over works: Regular. If each chapter is its own state, it's the

  set of paths through a graph, which is a narrower class than regular. If the
  story tracks state (inventory, "if you've visited the castle"), it's still
  regular, because the state is finite.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Work: Discworld chart
  Natural shape: Sub-series chains with crossover arrows, read in any order the
  arrows allow
  Language class over works: Finite, so trivially regular. But a regex for it is

  enormous (see below).

  You only get past regular with unbounded recursive nesting: side-quests that
  must return "to where you were" and can contain further side-quests. That is
  context-free. Your acyclicity invariant on nested sequences already rules this
  out, and real books bound the depth anyway.

  Why regex is still the wrong tool

  1. It loses choice points. The expressions ab | ac and a(b|c) describe the
  same set of readings, but they are different CYOA books. In the first, the
  reader commits before reading a. In the second, they choose after reading it.
  What the reader experiences is the branching structure, not the set of
  possible readings. So you need a transition system, not a language. Your step
  identity already gives you most of this. Steps are separate from their
  targets, so two steps can both target chapter a but offer different choices
  next. That is exactly the step from "paths in a graph" to a general automaton.

  2. It can't express "any order" compactly. The Discworld chart is a partial
  order, and its readings are its linear orderings. You could also allow
  readings of just its prefixes, if a reader may stop after the Witches books. A
  plain regex or automaton has to list every interleaving:
  - Take k independent chains of length n. The automaton needs roughly (n+1)^k
    states, one for each combination of positions across the chains.
  - For Discworld's sub-series plus its standalones, that's very roughly tens of
    thousands to millions of states. That's a back-of-envelope estimate, not a
    count.
  - Adding a shuffle operator (∥) to regex keeps you within regular but shrinks
    the description exponentially.

  Even shuffle plus nesting only gives you series-parallel orders. Those can't
  express an N-shaped crossover: a→c, b→c, b→d, but not a→d. I believe the chart
  has crossovers like that, though I haven't checked it arrow by arrow. If it
  does, nested unordered groups won't capture it, and you need explicit
  prerequisite edges.

  3. The two kinds of edge mean different things. A CYOA edge is an OR: after A,
  read B or C. A Discworld edge is an AND: reading A unlocks B and C, and you
  may read both. A single edge type can't carry both meanings.

  Minimum model

  Using the types in GroupsAndSequences.tex:

  type Flow  { steps: [Step], start: [Step], next: Step -> [Step], ends: [Step] 
  }  # reader picks one successor
  type Order { steps: [Step], before: [(Step, Step)] }                          
     # acyclic; any compatible order
  Step.target: Atom | Group | Flow | Order                                      
     # nesting, still acyclic

  - Your current Sequence is a special case of Flow: at most one next step each,
    with loopTo as a back-edge. Existing data carries over unchanged.
  - Rayuela: either one Flow with two entry steps, or two Flows over one group,
    as you have now.
  - CYOA: a Flow whose steps have several next steps and include cycles. Finite
    state like inventory gets encoded as distinct steps targeting the same atom.
    The cost is a bigger graph, not a new primitive. Explicit guards ("if you
    have the key") would only make it more compact.
  - Discworld: an Order whose steps target each sub-series, or one big Order
    over the books with the crossover edges.
  - Narnia ("Prince Caspian, then three in any order, then the rest"): a list
    whose middle step targets an Order with no edges. This removes the need,
    noted in the Revisited note, to mint a new container just for the three
    books.

  You don't need infinite-reading acceptance conditions (ω-automata). The
  AppView never decides whether a reading is valid. It only answers "from here,
  what may I read next?", and a finite graph with marked endings answers that.

  What this leaves out is anything mixing choice and concurrency, such as a
  choice in one sub-series that changes what another unlocks. That needs a
  Petri-net or event-structure model. None of your three examples requires it,
  so I'd leave it as an open question rather than build for it.

  I can draft this as a section in ReadingSequencesRevisted.tex, or as a
  replacement for the "Branching and optional steps" open question in
  GroupsAndSequences.tex.

⏺ Short answer: don't store Petri nets. There is a useful theorem behind your
  hunch, but the result that best suits a terse record is a different one: the
  Refined Process Structure Tree. It says a flow graph breaks down, uniquely,
  into a tree of nested blocks. That suggests storing a tree of blocks: plain
  sequence, AND and OR blocks for the common cases, and an explicit graph only
  for the leftover parts that don't break down. Petri nets then become the
  meaning an AppView uses to check records, not the format anyone writes.

  The theorems that exist

  Petri nets. The good theory covers free-choice nets. In these, whenever two
  transitions compete for a place, nothing else can enable one without the
  other. That restriction fits reading works: a choice depends only on where you
  are. Two results matter:

  - Hack's covering theorems (1972). Every well-behaved free-choice net is
    covered by:
    - state machines: every transition has one input and one output, so they are
      pure OR, i.e. automata, i.e. your Flow;
    - marked graphs: every place has one input and one output, so they are pure
      AND, i.e. partial orders, i.e. your Order.

    So, roughly, every sane mix of AND and OR is an overlay of Flows and Orders.
    The catch is that the covering pieces overlap and aren't nested. That makes
    it a theorem for checking structures, not for storing them.
  - Favre, Fahland and Völzer (2015). Graphs with only AND and XOR (exclusive
    OR) gateways correspond to free-choice workflow nets. Node-and-edge graphs
    with gateways are therefore already as expressive as the useful Petri nets,
    without making you store places explicitly. Explicit places roughly double
    what gets written, and they force authors to think in tokens.

  The Refined Process Structure Tree (Vanhatalo, Völzer and Koehler, 2008–09).
  It is built on Hopcroft–Tarjan triconnected components and SPQR trees:

  - Any flow graph with one start and one end, cycles included, has a unique
    tree of single-entry, single-exit fragments.
  - Each fragment is one of four kinds:
    - trivial: one edge;
    - polygon: a sequence;
    - bond: parallel branches, either AND or XOR, including loops;
    - rigid: an irreducible tangle, such as N-shaped crossovers or a messy CYOA
      graph.
  - The tree can be computed in linear time.

  This result suits storage. It says nested blocks plus an explicit-graph
  fallback for rigid pieces covers every case, and that each graph has exactly
  one such form. An AppView could even convert a submitted graph to that form.

  Proposed record shape

  One record per sequence. Each step has a local key that maps to a target. Step
  identity therefore survives edits, the same target can appear twice, and each
  long AT-URI is written only once.

  {
    "steps": { "pc": "at://…/caspian", "vdt": "at://…", "sc": "at://…", "mn":
  "at://…", "lb": "…", "lww": "…", "hhb": "…" },
    "body": ["pc", {"and": ["vdt", "sc", "mn"]}, "lb", "lww", "hhb"]
  }

  The block vocabulary:

  ┌───────────────────────┬──────────────────────────────────┬──────────────┐
  │         Form          │             Meaning              │ Kind in the  │
  │                       │                                  │     tree     │
  ├───────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────┼──────────────┤
  │ "key"                 │ read this step                   │ trivial      │
  ├───────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────┼──────────────┤
  │ [a, b, …]             │ in order                         │ polygon      │
  ├───────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────┼──────────────┤
  │ {"and": [...]}        │ all of them, any interleaving    │ AND bond     │
  ├───────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────┼──────────────┤
  │ {"or": [...]}         │ the reader picks one             │ XOR bond     │
  ├───────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────┼──────────────┤
  │ {"loop": [...]}       │ repeat forever (terminal)        │ loop bond    │
  ├───────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────┼──────────────┤
  │ {"flow": {start,      │ explicit choice graph, cycles    │ rigid, pure  │
  │ next, end}}           │ allowed                          │ OR           │
  ├───────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────┼──────────────┤
  │ {"order": {before}}   │ explicit prerequisite graph,     │ rigid, pure  │
  │                       │ must be acyclic                  │ AND          │
  └───────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────────┴──────────────┘

  Blocks nest anywhere, including as nodes inside flow and order. A nested node
  means "read this whole block, then continue." Applied to the examples:

  - Rayuela, order 2: ["c73", "c1", "c2", "c116", …, {"loop": ["c58", "c131"]}]
  - Narnia variant: shown above. Seven short keys.
  - CYOA: a flow. The adjacency list {"p1": ["p4", "p9"], "p9": ["p1", "end2"], 
    …} writes each edge once. That is the minimum for an automaton, and CYOA
    graphs really are rigid.
  - Discworld: if the sub-series stay independent, {"and": [[witches…], 
    [watch…], …]}. If crossovers create N-shapes, which I expect but haven't
    checked, use an order with the crossover edges, about 40 nodes and 50 edges.
    The sub-series chains can be inner [...] nodes to keep it short.

  The one rule that keeps this safe

  Rigid fragments must be pure: all OR (flow) or all AND (order). Mixing happens
  only through nesting.

  The reason is deadlock. Suppose a choice says "read ending A or B," and a
  later book requires both. Its AND-join can never fire, and the reader is
  stranded. The safe cases:

  - Properly nested blocks can't deadlock; they are sound by construction.
  - A pure flow can't deadlock. It can only have unreachable endings, which a
    reachability check catches.
  - A pure acyclic order can't deadlock.

  Only mixed rigid graphs need a real soundness check. That check is polynomial
  for free-choice nets, but it isn't something every AppView should have to
  implement.

  What the purity rule gives up is a rigid tangle where a choice in one strand
  changes what unlocks in another. That is the same case I suggested leaving out
  last time. If you ever need it, the extension is gateway steps with no target
  and explicit and/xor types, validated by translating to a free-choice net.
  The format grows without breaking.

  Two smaller decisions

  - "May stop anywhere" fits better as a flag on the sequence (accept prefixes)
    than as encoded skips. Readers dropping out of a sub-series is normal, not
    part of the structure. Genuinely optional steps can be {"or": ["x", []]},
    where the empty sequence means "skip."
  - A string mini-language like "pc (vdt & sc & mn) lb lww hhb" would be shorter
    still, but it needs a parser, and Lexicon schemas can't validate it.
    Lexicon does allow recursive unions (feed thread views nest that way), so
    the structured form is expressible. Check record-size and nesting-depth
    limits before relying on deep trees.
